## Check the same findings again

After applying an input, the chiropractor repeats the assessment that identified the target. The comparison concerns the same findings under comparable conditions. If the original findings are no longer present, as intended, this provides evidence supporting the working judgment that an adjustment occurred.

If the findings persist or change in an unexpected way, reconsider the interpretation of the pattern and the choice of input. If the comparison is unclear, the response remains uncertain. These findings guide whether to stop, revise the working hypothesis, or consider a different input when justified. An unchanged finding does not automatically call for more input.

## What the comparison tells us

An [adjustment](/model/philosophy/adjustment/) means that the input caused a change in neuromotor organization. The strength of the reassessment evidence depends on how reliably the findings indicate that change.

Calling the response a [successful adjustment](/model/philosophy/successful-adjustment/) also requires a reason to interpret the change as better function. The same assessment can inform that judgment; a separate symptom or everyday performance goal is not required. The criteria for improvement must be justified before seeing the result, rather than defining any disappearance of a finding as success.

Use the comparison set out in assessment: the aspects of control or coordination being judged, the relevant conditions, the earlier findings or other comparison, and the time of reassessment. Consider relevant functions made worse as well as those improved. A changed finding can leave the change in organization, its cause, or its benefit uncertain. Keep uncertain outcomes uncertain and include every delivered input when evaluating the strategy.

## Limits of the evidence

A before-and-after comparison alone does not establish that the input caused the change. Natural variability, repeated testing, expectation, measurement error, and other contextual influences remain alternative explanations. Immediate findings also do not establish the proposed neural mechanism, how long a change lasts, or broader functional benefits. The reliability and meaning of the assessment findings, and the criteria used to make decisions, still require independent evaluation.

An established functional benefit would still not require further inputs or show that the intervention was worth all its costs and risks. A favorable local result can coexist with relevant losses or better alternatives. Reassessment informs a fresh decision, including stopping, rather than creating a requirement to continue.
