## Why I’m Doing This

If we haven’t met, “Hi, I’m Dr. Matt\! I’m a self-proclaimed chiropractic nerd.” I’m that guy who reads books on chiropractic philosophy for fun, might randomly wake up at 2am with a thought about subluxation and reticulospinal tracts, and has been guilty more than once of being a mad scientist who uses friends and family members as unsuspecting guinea pigs for new adjusting techniques (after a full informed consent, of course\!).

While I’ve spent years thinking about and refining my application of the philosophy, science, and art of chiropractic, most of it is in my head. The goal of this project is twofold:

1. Get my ideas on paper (or on the screen). Writing them down helps me organize them. Making my ideas public means I need to make sure they make at least a little sense, and it makes me accountable for not letting this project die.  
2. Develop a coherent, defensible model of chiropractic that connects philosophy, science, and art, is guided by first principles and tested against reality, and makes the reasoning behind its conclusions visible.

## The Chiropractic Identity Crisis

The field of chiropractic is deeply fragmented. I’m sure you’ve noticed. We have some camps that say they are “principled,” while others identify themselves as “evidence-based.” These groups seem to disagree on just about everything, even though there is obviously no inherent conflict between philosophical principles and empirical evidence. Then there are the technique camps. Some chiropractors adjust full-spine, while others are upper cervical. Some chiropractors work on extremities, and still others don’t adjust at all. Even among those who agree on where we should adjust, there are divides of articular vs. orthogonal, tonal vs. structural, and on and on it goes.

All of this is before we bring up the “S” word. Subluxation is a term that has been hotly debated both inside and outside the profession since the dawn of chiropractic. Some argue that it doesn’t exist, while others use a multitude of different, and sometimes conflicting, definitions.

I could fill pages with all of the things that chiropractors disagree on, but that wouldn’t accomplish anything. What matters is that all this disagreement creates problems for the profession across multiple domains, including regulation, research, communication, technique, and public perception, just to name a few.

## The Tradeoff of Independent Thinking

Chiropractic has long attracted people willing to question prevailing assumptions. I think that independent streak is one of our greatest strengths, but it has a predictable consequence: independent thinkers will sometimes reach different conclusions.

The problem isn’t disagreement. The problem is when those disagreements remain unexamined. We can each build internally coherent ways of thinking without ever forcing them into contact with one another. That’s how independent thought turns into fragmentation.

**So how do you get lots of independently thinking chiropractors on the same page?**

Maybe you don’t. At least, not by asking them to agree.

A better place to start is by agreeing on how we disagree. If we claim to value logic, critical thinking, and good science, then our ideas should be open to examination. We should be able to identify our assumptions, explain the reasoning behind our models, show what evidence supports their empirical claims, and be willing to revise them when the reasoning or evidence doesn’t hold up.

This is where conflict becomes useful. B.J. Palmer titled a book _Conflicts Clarify_, and I think there is something important in that idea. Disagreement doesn’t have to divide us. Done well, it helps us figure out exactly where we part ways. Do we disagree about the premise? The logic? The evidence? Or are we simply using the same words to mean different things?

Independent thought without that kind of dialogue almost guarantees fragmentation. We each follow our own reasoning down separate paths and rarely stop long enough to compare maps.

## Making the Map Visible

That’s really what I want to build here: a map. Not a set of conclusions everyone is expected to accept, but a coherent model that makes its assumptions, reasoning, evidence, and path to practical decisions visible.

If we disagree about what chiropractic is or where it should go, I want us to be able to trace our paths backward and find the fork in the road. Maybe we started with different assumptions. Maybe one of us made a logical leap. Maybe the science points in a different direction than we thought. Or maybe we actually agree and are just using different language.

Of course, there is an obvious danger here.

![How Standards Proliferate Comit](https://imgs.xkcd.com/comics/standards.png "How Standards Proliferate Comit")

Chiropractic probably does not need someone announcing that he has finally created the 15th framework that will unify the other 14\. I’m not pretending to approach this without beliefs or biases. I have a model of chiropractic that I think is largely right, or I wouldn’t be building it. But my goal isn’t to pretend it is neutral or insist that everyone practice the same way. It is to make the assumptions, reasoning, evidence, and value judgments behind it visible enough to be challenged. You may look at that rationale and choose a very different model. If so, I hope this framework helps us identify exactly where we diverge.

I also want to point out that some of what I present will be wrong. I want to find those parts.

This model is supposed to change. I want it to be a living structure that gets revised as better arguments, better evidence, and better explanations emerge. If someone points out a faulty premise, weak evidence, or a conclusion that simply doesn’t follow, the goal isn’t to defend it. The goal is to fix it.

In fact, if this model looks exactly the same five years from now, something has probably gone wrong. My hope is that putting it in public makes it increasingly coherent and defensible over time, not because criticism gets ignored, but because good criticism gets incorporated.

## How I Plan to Build the Model

So what am I actually going to do?

I plan to build this model publicly as a living, interconnected map of chiropractic. As much as possible, I want each major conclusion to show what it depends on: the underlying assumptions or principles, the reasoning that connects them, the scientific evidence where empirical claims are involved, and the practical implications that follow. The articles on this site will let me explore individual pieces in more depth, while the model itself will represent my best current attempt to put those pieces together coherently.

A few principles will guide how I approach it:

* **Be guided by first principles.** Make assumptions explicit, trace them toward first principles, test their empirical implications against reality, and revise them when they don’t hold up.  
* **Seek truth, not validation.** The goal is not to defend what I already believe.  
* **Conflicts clarify.** Disagreement is useful when it helps us identify where our premises, reasoning, evidence, or values diverge.  
* **Integrate philosophy, science, and art.** Philosophy clarifies chiropractic’s questions, concepts, and purposes; science tests and refines its empirical claims; art puts that understanding into practice.  
* **Share information freely.** Ideas get better when people can examine them, challenge them, use them, and build upon them. I intend for this model to be freely accessible and shared.

The model itself will be more structured than these articles. Individual claims will eventually show what they depend on, the reasoning and evidence behind them, important objections or alternatives, how confident I am in them, and how they change over time. The goal is to make the reasoning traceable, not just present a collection of conclusions.

## Chiropractic Nerds, Unite\!

I know I’m not the only chiropractic nerd out there. If you’re interested in joining me on this journey, I invite you to participate. Comment. Challenge my assumptions. Point out weak logic, missing evidence, or places where the map doesn’t seem to match reality. All I ask is that we do it thoughtfully and respectfully. The point isn’t to win debates or sound smart to each other. It’s to find the gaps in our thinking and make the model better.

So that’s the experiment: build a model guided by first principles, test it against reality, put it where people can throw rocks at it, and see what survives.
