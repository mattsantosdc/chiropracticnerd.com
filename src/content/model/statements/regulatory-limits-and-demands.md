## Different kinds of limits

Incomplete information concerns what neural regulation can estimate about the body and environment. Biological constraints concern what responses are feasible, including the capacities of neural processes and the body they regulate. Competing demands concern priorities and tradeoffs when responses cannot satisfy every demand together. A response that serves one demand can restrict another.

These are distinct limitations. Knowing a condition accurately does not remove a limit on responding to it. A conflict between demands need not arise from sensory uncertainty, and not every biological constraint makes sensory information less accurate. The particular information, capacity, or conflict must be identified when explaining a response.

## Current evidence

The three components have complementary support:

- **Incomplete information:** [Faisal, Selen, and Wolpert (2008)](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn2258) synthesize evidence for noise from molecular processes through behavior. Sensory noise and limits on signal transmission restrict the information available for regulation, supporting uncertainty about bodily and environmental conditions rather than complete access to them.
- **Biological constraints:** The same review documents physiological variability in neural signaling and motor output. [Harris and Wolpert (1998)](https://doi.org/10.1038/29528) model motor commands with noise that increases with signal magnitude. The model predicts observed eye and arm movement characteristics and the speed–accuracy tradeoff, supporting noise as a constraint on achievable responses. This is agreement between a model and movement data, not proof that all regulation follows its optimization rule.
- **Competing demands:** [Trommershäuser, Maloney, and Landy (2003)](https://doi.org/10.1364/JOSAA.20.001419) studied five people making rapid reaches toward rewarded targets near penalty regions. Across two experiments, varying penalties and the overlap and number of penalty regions changed where participants aimed. With uncertain movement endpoints and a time limit, gaining reward competed with avoiding penalties. This directly demonstrates adjustment to competing objectives in the tested tasks; it does not establish that people always optimize those tradeoffs.

**Confidence is high.** Convergent evidence shows that neural regulation operates with noisy and therefore incomplete information, is constrained by properties of neural and motor systems, and adjusts behavior when objectives compete. These findings establish regulatory limits and tradeoffs, not generally poor regulation or an always-available better response. They provide no evidence that chiropractic can improve it. The specific source and consequences of a proposed limit still require function- and task-specific appraisal.

## Boundary

[The state-dependent motor-control claim](/model/science/state-dependent-neuromotor-function/) independently asserts context-dependent neuromotor function and limited or poorly suited strategies in some circumstances. The present limits account does not establish that every response is poorly suited, that a better feasible strategy exists, or that an accessible input could produce it. Even an accurate estimate can coexist with an unavoidable tradeoff. [The neuromotor-opportunity claim](/model/science/neuromotor-opportunity/) separately asserts beneficial modifiability in some cases; that commitment cannot be obtained from incomplete information or competing demands alone.
